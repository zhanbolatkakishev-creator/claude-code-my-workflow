## Major concerns

**1. The policy section's sharpest lever contradicts the paper's own sector ranking — and may not be Kazakhstan's to pull.** §11.1 "value-added or local-content thresholds for goods re-exported onward in the priority sectors": (a) `tab:priority` ranks the surge basket (computer/electronic/optical) as the *worst* place to invest (VA mult 0.69, near-zero base) — is the lever aimed at the surge basket (contradiction) or the substitution sectors (wrong instrument, those goods aren't transiting)? Never resolved. (b) Kazakhstan is in a customs union; whether a member can unilaterally impose value-added conditions on intra-EAEU onward supply is a live legal question the paper doesn't raise — plausibly no without Eurasian Economic Commission agreement. A lever the subject country can't pull isn't a policy implication. Scale never denominated (trades away 0.2–0.3% of GDP / $23–53m for a hypothetical plant).
→ *Change my mind:* state which product set the lever applies to and reconcile with `tab:priority`'s "Avoid" row; one paragraph on EAEU legal feasibility with a citation; put the trade-off in the paper's own units (throughput forgone vs. value a plant would add).

**2. §11.2 (corridor leverage) has no denominator and rests on facts absent from §5–§10.** "Close to an unavoidable node", "structural bargaining chip with China … and the EU" — asserted on Panama/Red Sea/Hormuz disruptions that appear nowhere in the empirics and carry no citation; then two paragraphs later conceded to be contestable (Iran/Türkiye, Azerbaijan, Georgia) and capacity-constrained (Caspian ferry, break-of-gauge), which guts the claim. Nothing in the data speaks to China–Europe transit — the measured object is a $479m flow to Russia.
→ *Change my mind:* give the corridor a denominator (Trans-Caspian tonnage/value vs. total China–EU trade and vs. KZ GDP) from a citable source; state the physical ceiling; re-scale or cut §11.2 to one flagged-conjectural paragraph.

**3. The institutional lever recommends changes to the same investor whose evidence is co-published by the author's employer, and names the employer's own exchange.** `qicaifcifc2026pe` (QIC/AIFC/IFC) supplies the §7–§8 state-fund evidence — one leg of the irreversibility argument — while §11.1 recommends changes to QIC's model. Disclosure footnote credited; this is a *weight* question. The report "gives no pre-2022 baseline and no sector-level denominator" (the paper's own §8) — it can support a qualitative observation, not a directional claim about QIC's allocation model, and §11.1 slides toward the latter. Separately: recommended exit = "trade sale or a listing on the Astana exchange"; AIFC operates AIX and employs the author — disclosure is not discipline. "No functioning exit, buy-back the leading route" appears twice with no citation/count/denominator.
→ *Change my mind:* restate the QIC evidence as strictly qualitative; generalise the exit recommendation (drop the named venue or support it with liquidity/listing evidence); source the buy-back claim with a route-level exit count from the deal data already held.

**4. Vietnam and Mexico are the framework's only out-of-sample support, and they are two sentences with no data.** §9 "Contrast cases" asserts structural reallocations + USMCA rules of origin + "both invested in capacity despite … not uniformly deep [financial markets]" with no investment number for either country, no evidence the RoO change (vs. tariff threat, wages, China-plus-one) induced the capacity, and one citation to a survey article. Ex-post selection because they match = illustration, not validation. Matters because the paper claims falsifiable content and this is the only place the positive half of the prediction is examined.
→ *Change my mind:* put numbers on the contrast cases (sector greenfield/FDI in Vietnamese electronics, Mexican autos, with a source; cite the USMCA RVC rule) — or demote the paragraph to explicitly labelled motivating illustration and stop calling it "the pattern the framework predicts."

**5. The population is named loosely, and the cheapest decisive comparator is missing.** Read strictly the conditions are {transit economy, customs union *with the shock destination*, financially shallow, policy-contingent reallocation} ≈ {Kazakhstan, Armenia, Kyrgyz Rep., Belarus}. The central mechanism is the *open* market-access gate and the paper never observes a case where it's *closed*. Georgia / Türkiye / UAE / Serbia got the same 2022 reallocation with **no** customs union with Russia — the framework predicts a supply response there, and the trade half of that comparison is in data the author already has. Armenia's GFCF rising ~3pp of GDP (the single most visible test of the prediction, running the wrong way) is dismissed in one clause; a construction-vs-tradeable-manufacturing decomposition would likely vindicate the paper.
→ *Change my mind:* named-population paragraph in §9; a trade-side surge-basket comparison with ≥1 non-customs-union intermediary (Georgia/Türkiye) with an explicit prediction and test; a two-paragraph treatment of Armenian GFCF.

**6. The case does not discriminate among the three gates — the paper says so in one place and denies it in another.** §8: the market-access gate is open and "close to sufficient for R ≈ 0 on its own" → the case is then uninformative about the other two gates (`tab:moderators` shows KZ adverse on 6 of 7 moderators). Yet §8 also claims the irreversibility gate is "independently adverse" on a comparison it lists three reasons not to trust. Normative consequence (this referee's to own): `tab:magnitudes` shows exports to Russia flat at ~10× baseline through 2025 and §9 concedes "Kazakhstan's persist" — if ρ is high, the entire §11.1 "Irreversibility" lever block addresses a non-problem and the conclusion inverts to "market access alone explains the null" (a sharper paper). Also §10's ranking has no cost side — a VA multiplier is not a rate of return, and an import/output ratio of 5.6–7.9 is as consistent with comparative disadvantage as with "headroom"; the "combined score" is an unweighted ad hoc index.
→ *Change my mind:* state plainly in §8/§12 that the case identifies an open market-access gate and does not rank the other two (drop "independently adverse" unless the pipeline evidence is strengthened); add a paragraph on how §11.1 changes if ρ is high; add a cost/return dimension to §10 or caveat it as a gross-VA ordering.

**7. Literature positioning thin (19 refs) with three substantive omissions.** (a) Feenstra–Hanson on Hong Kong re-exports of Chinese goods — canonical estimation of exactly the entrepôt margin §6 calibrates — absent. (b) Nothing from the EAEU/Customs Union integration literature (Isakova–Koczan–Plekhanov, Tarr, Vinokurov) in a paper whose main mechanism is a customs union. (c) The dynamic-gains argument runs through `desouza2024diffusion` alone; the FDI-spillover literature (Javorcik and successors) is the natural companion for the "corridor forgoes learning" point. Minor: `khanna1997` is the HBR piece.
→ *Change my mind:* add and *engage* the entrepôt-margin literature in §6, the EAEU literature in §2/§3, the FDI-spillover literature in §3(iii)/§12.

## Recommendation: **Major revision (R&R)**

Enough here for JCE to want the paper — a genuine gap, careful measurement, clean reproducibility, unusually honest self-limitation. What it lacks is the second half of its own bar: a sharp framework applied to a case that teaches something *general*. Most of the gap is cheap to close with data in hand: a non-customs-union comparator (Georgia/Türkiye) on the existing construction, a denominator for the corridor claim, a decomposition of Armenian GFCF, an exit-route count from the deal extract, and a disciplining pass over §11 forcing every recommendation onto a named gate and a §5–§8 number.
