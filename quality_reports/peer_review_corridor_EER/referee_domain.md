# Domain Referee Report

**Calibrated to:** European Economic Review (EER), special issue "Global Trade Fragmentation and Regional Trade Alliances"
**Disposition:** SKEPTIC
**Critical peeve:** Any claim about "policy implications" must be supported by the data's support range (targets: Sections 10–12, levers 11.1–11.3, Table 5 ranking, the 2026 manufacturing-expansion argument)
**Constructive peeve:** A clear "what this paper does not show" paragraph that honestly bounds the claims
**Date:** 2026-10-05

## Executive verdict

**Score:** 61/100
**Recommendation:** Reject (borderline; see the closing note to the editor)
**Headline:** The positive results are carefully and honestly bounded. But the central "missing investment response" is close to what the nature of the shock predicts on its own, so the question is close to a strawman. The policy half of the paper (Sections 10–12) then makes recommendations for gates and sectors that the evidence does not reach, and in places it contradicts the paper's own findings.

## Major concerns

### Concern 1: The null is what the shock's own nature predicts, so the "textbook expectation" it is tested against is a strawman (potentially fatal to the framing)

**Dimension:** 1 (also 4)
**Severity:** MAJOR (closest to fatal)

**Description:**
- The Introduction motivates the paper with the textbook expectation that a sharp, persistent demand shock is "acted on by investment" (Sec. 1, para 1). The cited analogues are Juhász (2018) and the import-substitution logic. In those settings, though, the shock cut off supply to the home market.
- Here the demand sits in Russia. It is $119–145m a year spread across 29 HS6 lines, roughly $4–5m per line (Table 1). Twenty-four of the 29 lines come from the EU/US/UK/JP Common High Priority Items list (Table B.1 note), mostly HS 8542/8517/8532/8541 components.
- Three things make a Kazakh plant implausible from the start:
  - **Minimum efficient scale.** The minimum efficient scale for integrated circuits, transceivers or capacitors is orders of magnitude above this flow.
  - **Sanctions exposure.** Any Kazakh producer selling these items to Russia would itself be exposed to secondary sanctions and export controls on its own inputs.
  - **Location inside the union.** Inside the customs union, the natural place to localise production for Russian demand is Russia, which has the market, the scale and the existing base, not the transit partner.
- Kazakhstan's comparative advantage in this flow is *jurisdictional*: it can procure goods that Russia cannot. It is not productive.
- The paper does list "cross-border compliance and reputational exposure" (Sec. 2, Sec. 7). It treats this as one of three confounds that "over-determine" the null. In my view it is the first-order explanation, and it sits outside the three-gate framework altogether, effectively as a fourth, legal gate. The paper's own concession that "an absent industry is equally what a customs union with no comparative advantage in the sector would produce" (Sec. 3, Sec. 8) points the same way.
- The paper consistently uses the word "reorientation". The policy section also never confronts the fact that the flow it analyses is, in large part, the flow that Western export controls aim to stop. Both choices obscure the most plausible explanation for the null.

**Why this matters:** If the null is predicted by scale, sanctions-legality and within-union location, the paper has no puzzle to explain. The general lesson for EER readers ("customs-union membership turns demand shocks into transit") is then not identified separately from "nobody builds sanctioned components for a sanctioned buyer".

**What would change my mind:** Show that the paper's core claim survives once these explanations are dealt with. Any one of the following would do:
- (a) Re-run the retention and investment analysis on a reorientation flow that is not sanctions-exposed and where minimum efficient scale is plausible relative to the flow. Candidates are civilian consumer goods, vehicles and parts, or machinery re-exported to Russia, all of which the cited Chupilkin et al. literature documents. Show that the same corridor-not-factory pattern holds there.
- (b) Write down, before looking at the outcome, which investment response the framework would have predicted for this specific flow. Then demonstrate that a reasonable observer in 2022 expected capacity to follow.
- (c) Reframe the paper explicitly as a study of sanctions-induced transit and its domestic content, with the legal gate as part of the framework.

### Concern 2: The "no investment response" claim is stronger than the instrument can support

**Dimension:** 3
**Severity:** MAJOR (addressable)

**Description:**
- The investment outcome is a count of M&A/PE/VC transactions in a broad sector bundle: manufacturing of tradeable goods, transport and distribution. That is about 7–8 deals a year, N = 82 over 11 years (Table 3).
- On power, the paper is honest (Sec. 7): the test has 80% power only against a rise of about 80% or more.
- Deal counts are, however, a poor proxy for capacity formation. Greenfield capacity built by incumbents, Russian relocations or Chinese firms without an external transaction is invisible. The paper concedes this in Limitation 4. The surge-basket line in Table 3 is 0 before and after, so it carries no information (as the paper itself says).
- The abstract nonetheless states "Across three deal databases we find no investment response". The Discussion states "no detectable investment response". Kazakhstan's statistical bureau publishes investment in fixed capital by activity and enterprise registrations by activity. The paper uses the bureau's input–output and resources tables but not these series. New-firm entry in wholesale trade of electronics would also be a direct test of the mediation story (Sec. 3) that re-export activity M expanded instead of capacity.

**Why this matters:** The title and abstract present a null, but what the paper actually shows is "no detectable change in deal counts in a broad sector, powered only against a near-doubling". A reader cannot tell whether the investment null is substantive or an artefact of the data.

**What would change my mind:**
- Add official fixed-capital investment by activity, at the division level for C26 computers/electronics, C27 electrical equipment, G46 wholesale and H52 warehousing, for 2015–2025, with an event-style comparison.
- Add firm-registration counts for the same activities.
- Rewrite the abstract to state the detectable effect size.

### Concern 3: The policy sections (10–11) recommend levers outside the data's support range, and in places contradict the paper's own findings (critical peeve)

**Dimension:** 3 (also 4)
**Severity:** MAJOR (addressable by substantial cutting, not by new data)

**Description:** The Limitations section is admirably clear that the case observes "a single configuration, in which the three gates are not separately identified" and that the irreversibility gate is "undetermined". Section 11 then prescribes levers for all three gates:

- **Irreversibility lever (11.1).** It proposes anchor and off-take contracts, demand aggregation, shared pilot lines and co-financing. These answer a gate the paper says it cannot show binds. No evidence in the paper bears on whether lowering σ or I would change entry.
- **Market-access lever (11.1).** "A standing rule that flows above a threshold trigger a domestic-content review" has no support in any data the paper observes: the paper never sees a closed gate for the re-export flow. The paper itself notes such a rule conflicts with EAEU free-movement rules. Applied to "the next shock of this kind", it would also amount to advising a government on how to capture more domestic value from transit that export controls target. The paper does not discuss this, and EER readers will notice. The local-content literature on the efficiency costs of such rules is not engaged (Grossman 1981, QJE, on domestic-content protection).
- **Institutional lever (11.1, Sec. 8 last paragraph, Sec. 10).** The claim is that adjacent durable opportunities (logistics, machinery, fabricated metal) "go unfinanced by private capital" and are "financed only slowly and only by directed state capital". The paper presents no investment data on these sectors. The claim that their irreversibility gate is "plausibly open" is asserted. The only sector-level private evidence (Table 3) is flat deal counts. The exit-preference evidence comes from a survey report co-produced by the state investment corporation whose restructuring the lever recommends. That is a source-independence issue the paper should at least acknowledge.
- **Table 5 / Figure 5.** The ranking rests on domestic value-added multipliers between 0.79 and 0.82 for every recommended sector. These are 2019 ICIO coefficients with no uncertainty attached, and differences of 0.01–0.03 cannot support an ordering.
  - The multiplier is value added per dollar of *output*, not per dollar *invested*. The Figure 5 caption "Where investment buys the most domestic value added" therefore does not follow; capital intensity and returns are absent.
  - The "import-substitution gap" (imports/output) is read as headroom. Section 3 explicitly says a near-zero domestic base is "equally what a customs union with no comparative advantage in the sector would produce". The ranking interprets as opportunity the very statistic the paper says it cannot interpret.
  - The combined score's weights, and the "deal base" component, are not shown. Only 8 of 13 sectors appear.
  - "Wholesale & retail trade infrastr." carries gross output of $81bn. That is the whole trade sector, not infrastructure, so the "logistics platform" recommendation borrows a retail-inclusive multiplier.
  - "The corridor's binding constraint" (Sec. 10) is supported only by a 2010 pre-shock citation.
- **China levers (11.3).** These are justified because "China supplies about two-thirds of the inbound flow". But the paper's own measurement says the China flow was pre-existing and is *not* the reorientation (Sec. 4, Sec. 5.1, Sec. 6.1 use the Western component for that reason). The paper flags these levers as untested hypotheses, which is good, but their motivation contradicts its own decomposition.
- **Corridor leverage (11.2).** No analysis in the paper bears on Kazakhstan's bargaining position. The section reads as commentary.

**Why this matters:** EER's profile explicitly excludes "policy advocacy without an analytical spine". The positive results support roughly one policy statement: transit through a customs union with the destination captures little domestic value, so throughput is not industrial policy. Everything beyond that is extrapolation from an n = 1 configuration.

**What would change my mind:**
- Cut Section 11 to the statements the evidence supports, and label the rest as conjecture in one short paragraph.
- Either remove the Table 5 ranking or support it with (i) investment-based rather than output-based value added, (ii) uncertainty or sensitivity of the ranking to weights and to the BNS 2023 table, and (iii) some evidence that the substitution sectors are not comparative-disadvantage sectors, for example revealed comparative advantage or unit-cost comparisons.
- Provide actual investment data for the "adjacent opportunities" before claiming they go unfinanced.

### Concern 4: The 2026 manufacturing-expansion argument is unfalsifiable as used, and contradicts Table 5 and the institutional-gate claim

**Dimension:** 3
**Severity:** MAJOR (addressable)

**Description:** Section 12 uses January–July 2026 industrial-output shares from a press report, outside the 2022–2025 sample. It reads them as "exactly the configuration under which the framework predicts a supply response". Three problems:

- **No evidence for the mechanism.** The attribution to local-content shares, mandatory procurement and off-take contracts is asserted. No evidence links sector growth to coverage by these measures, and the paper itself notes the denominator effect from falling oil output.
- **Contradiction with Table 5.** The growth is "concentrated in passenger vehicles and assembly". Table 5 classifies motor vehicles as "Avoid" (kit assembly, 0.69). Section 11.1 says the market-access lever should steer investment "away from low-value final assembly". The headline evidence that the closed-gate lever works is the sector the paper tells policymakers to avoid.
- **Contradiction with the institutional-gate claim.** Machinery (+22%), fabricated metals (+35%), chemicals (+26.9%) and pharmaceuticals (+38.5%) are the very "substitution" sectors that Sections 8, 10 and 11.1 describe as going "unfinanced" and "financed only slowly".

The capacity figures also need reconciling: about 190,000 units of announced capacity in Sec. 12, against about 90,000 units plus a KIA plant and Škoda assembly in Sec. 8. Announced capacity is not output.

**Why this matters:** If the framework accommodates both a null (re-export) and an expansion (manufacturing) after the fact, with no observation that could have contradicted it, the "organising device" has no empirical content. The paragraph also undermines Section 10.

**What would change my mind:**
- Either drop the paragraph, or show a sector-level cross-tabulation (2022–2026) of output or investment growth against a pre-defined indicator of local-content/off-take coverage. The indicator should be specified before looking at growth.
- Reconcile the paragraph with the "Avoid" classification and with the "unfinanced adjacent opportunities" claim.

### Concern 5: The value-capture headline rests on a calibration that sets aside the paper's own evidence of scarcity rents

**Dimension:** 1 / 3
**Severity:** MAJOR (addressable)

**Description:**
- The "5–11 cents per rerouted dollar" is, as the paper candidly says, m itself (Sec. 6.2, Table D.1). The input–output step does no work.
- The band m = 6–14% is chosen as a wholesale-and-freight convention. Yet the paper's own f.o.b. wedge has tier medians near 4 in Tiers 2 and 4A, covering "about half the matched gross flow" (Sec. 6.1). It interprets this, plausibly, as a scarcity markup. A scarcity markup earned by a Kazakh-resident intermediary *is* retained domestic value added, in the form of operating surplus. It is not a reason to fall back on a normal wholesale margin.
- The censored matched-cell margin of 34% is treated as an artefact, and that reconciliation is admittedly incomplete (App. D).
- Who retains the rent also matters for the "corridor" reading. If the intermediaries are relocated Russian-owned firms, the value added is domestic in GDP but not in GNI terms.
- The benchmark "against three-quarters for a produced dollar" is rhetorical, not a counterfactual. Kazakhstan was never going to produce these dollars (Concern 1). The relevant comparison is rerouting versus no rerouting.

**Why this matters:** The title's quantitative core rests on a margin chosen against the direction of the paper's own price evidence. In a sanctions-evasion market, rents are exactly where one would expect retention to be *high*.

**What would change my mind:**
- Bring in any direct evidence on intermediary margins or profits: firm financial statements of the trading firms in the relevant wholesale class, customs-declaration-level unit values, or exporter counts and ownership from the business register.
- Report the headline as a band that includes the scarcity-rent case, or explain why rents accrue offshore.
- Replace "versus a produced dollar" with "versus the no-rerouting counterfactual" as the main comparison.

## Minor suggestions

- **Strengthen positioning against the US–China connector-country literature.** This is the closest comparison and it is under-cited. Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy, Khandelwal and Taglioni (2024, AER: Insights) and Freund, Mattoo, Mulabdic and Ruta (2024, JIE) on reallocation through Vietnam and Mexico are the natural foils for the Section 9 contrast cases, which currently rest on a single industrial-policy survey (Juhász et al., 2024). Freund et al. find that gaining countries deepened ties with China, which complicates the "structural vs corridor" contrast. Gopinath, Gourinchas, Presbitero and Topalova on "connector" countries is also relevant. (I am confident of the first two; I have not verified the final venue and year of the third.)
- **Engage the market-access literature directly.** The "market-access gate" is close to tariff-jumping FDI (Blonigen 2002, JIE) and to rules-of-origin sourcing effects (Conconi, García-Santana, Puccio and Venturini 2018, AER). Engaging these would sharpen the claimed novelty of the gate.
- **Narrow basket.** The 29-line basket is drawn from 75 pre-chosen candidates. The literature documents reorientation across a much broader product range (magnitude not verified here). Report how much of Kazakhstan's total incremental exports to Russia the basket covers, so readers can judge whether "Kazakhstan is a corridor" is a claim about the country or about 29 lines.
- **Customs-duty estimate (Sec. 6.3).** Many HS 8542/8517 lines may be duty-free under ITA commitments; I have not verified this for the EAEU CET. If so, the $22m is an upper bound, which only strengthens the point.
- **Cross-references.** Appendix F says the searches are referenced in "Sections 7 and 9" and cites the "auto-sector triangulation in Section 7". Both are in Section 8. Section 3 refers to "observation (ii)/(iii)", but the four observations are not visibly numbered in the extracted text; this may be an extraction artefact.
- **Equations.** Equations (1) and (2) came through garbled: underbrace labels run inline and the parentheses in VT are unbalanced. I did not evaluate their typesetting. (Orchestrator note: this is an artefact of the plain-text extraction of the Word file's native equations, not of the Word file itself.)
- **Readability.** The Introduction carries permutation-test and bootstrap detail (para 3) that a general EER reader does not need. The density of qualifications throughout makes the core claim hard to find. One clean statement of each result, with caveats collected in Limitations, would read better.
- **QIC as an "unconstrained investor".** The paper's own figures complicate this: $120m deployed in 2024, $1.0bn of 2025's $1.5bn in a single Qarmet bond, and a mandate focused on domestic food, energy and infrastructure. A state corporation's non-entry into sanctions-exposed components is not informative about capital-market institutions. Soften "inconsistent with the institutional gate" to "uninformative about".
- **The 2026 statistics.** These come via a newspaper report of statistical-bureau data; cite the primary release.

## Positive observations

1. **Honest bounding, the paper's best feature.** The Limitations section, Section 8 and Appendix F come close to the "what this paper does not show" paragraph I look for:
   - The paper states that the three gates are not separately identified and that the irreversibility gate is undetermined.
   - It states that the Türkiye/Georgia public-record search "is therefore not supportive of the prediction, not consistent with it". Authors rarely concede that a weak-instrument null fails to confirm their own prediction rather than claiming it as consistent.
   - The external-validity claim is explicitly narrowed: "the demand shock generalises... the investment null is established only for Kazakhstan".
2. **Retreat from the DiD magnitude.** The paper does not read the DiD γ as an identified magnitude. It shows the rule-matched permutation (p = 0.58), removes stars from the surge-basket row, and moves the identification burden to break dates and neighbours. It also adds the purged residual-list test. That is unusually disciplined.
3. **Transparency on the calibration.** The margin sweep (Table D.1) lets readers substitute their own m. The paper states plainly that the input–output step "does almost no work". The Section 7 power statement and the pre-stated coding rule for a positive investment response are also commendable.

## Note to the editor (fatal vs addressable)

- **Closest to fatal:** Concern 1. Concern 1(a) is the hard one: if the corridor-not-factory result is shown to hold on a non-sanctioned, scale-feasible reorientation flow, the paper becomes much more interesting for EER.
- **Addressable by cutting:** Concerns 3 and 4. Removing the overreach would leave a shorter and more credible paper.
- **Addressable with available data:** Concerns 2 and 5.

As it stands, the positive half is a careful descriptive note on a flow the EER literature (Chupilkin et al., 2026) has already established. The normative half outruns the evidence. If the guest editors see special-issue value in the customs-union angle, a Major Revision conditional on Concerns 1 and 3 would be the minimum I could support.
